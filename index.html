Tourism is back, but unevenly

Australian tourism has recovered on paper. Underneath, the picture is more uneven.

Since the borders reopened, is Australian tourism back? This story follows the recovery through visitor numbers, travel patterns, jobs and businesses, and asks three questions along the way. Have visitors actually returned? Who is coming now, and who is not? And who is benefiting from the recovery, in jobs and in businesses?

1. Jobs are well above 2019, but visitors are not

Start with the headline gap. By the second quarter of 2026, tourism employment is about 28% above its level in the same quarter of 2019, yet international visitor arrivals are still about 10% below it. Australians travelling overseas are above 2019 as well, so something other than inbound visitors is keeping the industry busy. The rest of this story looks for what.

Arrivals are calendar-quarter totals and jobs are quarter-ending estimates. The lines are aligned by quarter, but they measure different things. Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Overseas Arrivals and Departures and Tourism Labour Statistics.

2. The jobs came back long before the visitors did

Plotting the two together shows the order of events. Tourism jobs were near or above their old level while international arrivals were still recovering, and they kept climbing as visitors slowly returned. Employment, it seems, is not simply following the number of international arrivals, so the next question is who is arriving now.

Visitors are 12-month totals and jobs are the 12-month average of quarterly estimates. Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Overseas Arrivals and Departures and Tourism Labour Statistics.

3. New Zealand now leads China in visitors

The first answer is in the ranking. New Zealand (1.42 million visitors) has overtaken China (1.13 million), which was the largest source market in 2019, a lead of about 300,000 visitors. China is 22% below 2019 while South Korea is 45% above it, so this is not a general slowdown: some markets have shrunk and others have grown.

5. North-East Asia explains most of the visitors still missing

Zooming out from countries to regions shows where the gap sits. North-East Asia accounts for about 0.28 million of the 0.36 million visitor shortfall against 2019. Europe and Oceania are back to roughly 2019 levels, and growth from South and Central Asia offsets part of the loss, but it is not enough to fill the biggest missing region.

6. The December peak survived the shutdown, but the quiet months are still quieter

The gap is not even across the year either. December 2025 reached 96% of its 2019 level and February 2026 slightly passed its old peak, but June is still at 86%. Peak-season demand is close to normal while the quieter months lag, so the recovery is arriving in bursts rather than evenly.

Numbers in the squares are thousands of visitors. Orange outlines mark each year's busiest month; 2026 covers January to June only, so its outline will probably move. The most recent month is often revised. Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Overseas Arrivals and Departures.

7. Fewer holidaymakers and short stays, more workers and long stays

So who is filling the gap? The mix of visitors has changed. Holiday and business travel have fallen, while employment-related visits are up 81% and stays of six to twelve months are up 18%. Visitors are coming for work more than for holidays and staying longer, which may be how fewer arrivals can still support so much activity.

8. Seven of twelve tourism industries now employ more people than in 2019

That change in who visits shows up in who is hired. Accommodation, sports and recreation, and cafes and restaurants are well above their 2019 employment, while travel agencies and casinos are still deeply below. The winners are mostly industries that serve people who stay and do things, which fits with the longer-stay pattern above.

9. Accommodation has 55% more jobs than in 2019, but fewer businesses

More jobs do not automatically mean more businesses. Accommodation has expanded employment while its business count has slightly fallen, suggesting larger or more productive operators. Travel agencies show the opposite strain, keeping their businesses while losing jobs. Cafes, restaurants, sports and cultural services are the strongest broad-based growers.

10. Part-time work has grown fastest, especially for men

Looking inside the jobs themselves, employment has grown for both men and women, but mostly in part-time work. Men’s part-time jobs rose 39%, lifting their share of male tourism employment, while women’s part-time share stayed around two-thirds. The recovery is adding flexible work more than conventional full-time positions.

11. Business numbers grew in every state, but Sydney and Melbourne hold almost half of them

The same unevenness appears in where businesses are. The gains are strongest in the smaller jurisdictions, with the ACT leading state growth at 15%, but Sydney and Melbourne together hold 45% of regional businesses. The recovery is geographically widespread but economically concentrated in the big cities.

12. Tourism has fewer very small businesses and more large ones

Finally, the size of the businesses. Very small tourism businesses fell 15%, while businesses earning $2 million or more grew by roughly a third. The sector is becoming less dominated by microbusinesses and more reliant on larger operators, although the middle turnover group is still the largest.

So, is tourism back?

Back to the three questions. Tourism’s recovery is real, but it is not a return to the old pattern.

  • Have visitors returned? Not fully. Jobs and Australians’ overseas travel are above 2019, but international arrivals are still below it, and peak months are close to normal while the quiet months lag.
  • Who is coming now? New Zealand has overtaken China, and South Korea and India are above 2019, but North-East Asia accounts for most of the shortfall. The visitors who have come back are more likely to work or stay longer.
  • Who is benefiting? Accommodation, restaurants and experiences, part-time workers, and larger businesses, with business numbers concentrated in Sydney and Melbourne.
