Tourism is back, but unevenly

Tourism jobs are well above 2019, but international visitors are not

Tourism jobs and Australians returning from overseas are above their 2019 levels, while overseas visitor arrivals remain below. Each point compares a quarter with the same quarter in 2019, so 100 means “the same as that quarter in 2019”.

Arrivals are calendar-quarter totals and jobs are quarter-ending estimates. The lines are aligned by quarter, but they measure different things. Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Overseas Arrivals and Departures and Tourism Labour Statistics.

Jobs recovered long before visitors did

Each dot is one year of tourism, ending at a quarter. Moving right means more overseas visitors; moving up means more tourism jobs. Follow the line from the pre-COVID dot on the right, down through the border closures, and back up.

Visitors are 12-month totals and jobs are the 12-month average of quarterly estimates. Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Overseas Arrivals and Departures and Tourism Labour Statistics.

New Zealand has overtaken China as Australia’s biggest source of visitors

In 2019, China and New Zealand were almost level at about 1.4 million visitors each. Now New Zealand leads by about 300,000. South Korea and the Philippines each climbed two places, while Germany and the Netherlands each slipped two. Lines are coloured as on the map below.

Places are ranked among these 25 markets only, by number of visitors. Calendar 2019 compared with the 12 months from July 2025 to June 2026. Visitors are counted by country of residence. Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Overseas Arrivals and Departures.

Australia’s visitor map has been redrawn

Each curve runs from one of the 25 countries that sent the most visitors in the year to June 2026 (89% of all visitors) to Australia. Thicker curves mean more visitors. Blue curves are above their 2019 numbers, red curves are below. China sent 1.13 million visitors, 22% fewer than in 2019, while South Korea (+45%) and India (+9%) are above.

Visitors are counted by country of residence for the 12 months from July 2025 to June 2026, compared with calendar 2019. Curves start at each country’s capital city and end at the centre of Australia; they show direction and size, not flight paths. Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Overseas Arrivals and Departures. Map outlines: Natural Earth via the world-atlas package.

North-East Asia explains most of the visitors still missing

Each band is a region of origin, and its thickness is the number of visitors in the 12 months up to that date. Across all regions, 0.36 million fewer visitors arrived in the year to June 2026 than in 2019. North-East Asia, which includes China, Japan and South Korea, accounts for about 0.28 million of that gap. Oceania and Europe are back to 2019 levels, and South & Central Asia, which includes India, is 0.08 million above.

Visitors are counted by country of residence. Each point is the total for the 12 months ending that month, so the shapes lag the border dates slightly. Labels show the year to June 2026, with the change from calendar 2019 in brackets (millions). Europe combines North-West and Southern & Eastern Europe; Africa & Middle East combines North Africa and the Middle East with Sub-Saharan Africa. Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Overseas Arrivals and Departures.

The December peak survived the shutdown, but the quiet months are still quieter

Each square is one month of overseas visitors, and darker means more. Before COVID, December was always the busiest month, with about 1.08 million visitors in 2019, and February came second. By December 2025 the peak was back to 1.04 million, 96% of 2019, and February 2026 (943,000) edged past February 2019 (927,000). Winter months such as June are lagging more: June 2026 is 86% of June 2019.

Numbers in the squares are thousands of visitors. Orange outlines mark each year's busiest month; 2026 covers January to June only, so its outline will probably move. The most recent month is often revised. Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Overseas Arrivals and Departures.

Visitors now come to work and stay longer, not to take short trips

Compared with 2019, the year to June 2026 has about 4% fewer overseas visitors, but the mix has changed. Visitors coming for employment are up 81% and long stays of 6 to 12 months are up 18%, while business trips are down 27% and stays of under a week are down 14%. Holidaymakers are still the biggest group, at 4.13 million, 8% below 2019.

Seven of twelve tourism industries now employ more people than in 2019

Each row is one industry, compared with its own average for 2019. Blue means more jobs than in 2019 and red means fewer; darker shades mean further away. Accommodation has grown the most, at 71% above 2019, followed by sports and recreation (+53%) and cafes and restaurants (+34%). Travel agencies (−38%) and casinos (−36%) have not recovered. The grey band marks the period the borders were largely closed.

Each shade step is 30 percentage points from the 2019 average, up to 90 points. Figures at the right are the latest quarter (June 2026) against the 2019 average. Jobs are main jobs in tourism-related industries, counted at quarter end. Casinos and gambling is very small, at about 1,400 jobs, so its percentages move easily. Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Tourism Labour Statistics.

Accommodation has 55% more jobs than in 2019, but fewer businesses

This chart joins two data sources: tourism jobs and tourism businesses. Each bubble is an industry. Moving right means more businesses, and moving up means more jobs. Accommodation sits top-left, with jobs up 55% on 2019 while the number of businesses is down 2% since 2020. Cafes and restaurants, sports and recreation, clubs and cultural services all grew on both measures. Passenger transport and vehicle hire has 17% fewer businesses, driven by taxis (down 24%) while vehicle hire businesses are up 64%. Travel agencies kept their businesses (about 9,000) but lost 36% of their jobs.

The two measures cover different periods, because the business counts start in 2020: jobs compare the 2025 average with the 2019 average, and businesses compare 2025 with 2020. Matching industries are approximate. Passenger transport and vehicle hire combines taxis, motor vehicle hire and passenger transport businesses with road, air and water transport jobs. Retail trade is a "connected" industry, where only part of the output is tourism. Bubble area shows the number of businesses in 2025. Sources: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Tourism Labour Statistics (jobs); tourism business counts by industry (businesses).

Part-time work has grown fastest, especially for men

Men are on the left and women on the right, with full-time work next to the centre line and part-time work on the outside. Jobs for both sexes are now well above their 2019 levels (the dashed lines): men by 27% and women by 18% in the year to June 2026. Men's part-time jobs grew 39% between 2019 and 2025, against 18% for men's full-time jobs, so part-time work is now 45% of men's tourism jobs, up from 41%. For women the part-time share has stayed at about 63–66%.

Bars are the average number of main jobs over each calendar year; the last row is the four quarters to June 2026. Numbers at the end of each bar are total jobs for that sex. The dashed lines mark 2019. Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Tourism Labour Statistics.
