Tourism is back, but unevenly

Tourism is recovering, but not in the same way everywhere.

This story follows Australia's tourism recovery through visitor numbers, travel patterns, jobs and businesses. Together, the data shows which parts of the industry have returned to normal, which are still lagging, and how tourism is being reshaped after the pandemic.

Tourism jobs are well above 2019, but international visitors are not

Tourism employment has recovered faster than international demand. Australians are travelling overseas again and tourism businesses are hiring, but inbound arrivals remain below 2019, suggesting the industry is being supported by a changing mix of domestic and international activity.

Arrivals are calendar-quarter totals and jobs are quarter-ending estimates. The lines are aligned by quarter, but they measure different things. Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Overseas Arrivals and Departures and Tourism Labour Statistics.

Jobs recovered long before visitors did

The labour market rebounded before visitor numbers did. Tourism jobs were already near or above their former level while international arrivals were still recovering, showing that employment can return through a different mix of visitors, longer stays and changing industry demand.

Visitors are 12-month totals and jobs are the 12-month average of quarterly estimates. Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Overseas Arrivals and Departures and Tourism Labour Statistics.

New Zealand has overtaken China as Australia’s biggest source of visitors

Australia's visitor base has shifted toward nearby and emerging Asian markets. New Zealand now leads China by about 300,000 visitors, while South Korea and the Philippines have gained ground; the recovery is therefore broadening rather than simply returning to the old market order.

Australia’s visitor map has been redrawn

The recovery is uneven across countries. China remains well below its former contribution, while South Korea and India have already exceeded 2019 levels. The collapse in 2020–21 was global, but the return since 2023 has created a new pattern of strong and weak source markets rather than restoring the previous map.

North-East Asia explains most of the visitors still missing

North-East Asia is the main reason inbound tourism has not fully recovered: it accounts for about 0.28 million of the 0.36 million visitor shortfall. Europe and Oceania have returned to roughly 2019 levels, while growth from South and Central Asia partly offsets the continuing weakness in Australia's largest missing region.

The December peak survived the shutdown, but the quiet months are still quieter

Peak-season demand is close to normal, but the recovery is weaker outside summer. December 2025 reached 96% of its 2019 level and February 2026 slightly surpassed its old peak, while June remained at 86%; tourism is therefore recovering in bursts rather than evenly across the year.

Fewer holidaymakers and short stays, more workers and long stays

International tourism is becoming more work-oriented and long-stay. Holiday and business travel have fallen, while employment-related visits rose 81% and stays of six to twelve months increased 18%; even with fewer arrivals overall, visitors are contributing through longer and more purposeful trips.

Seven of twelve tourism industries now employ more people than in 2019

Tourism's recovery is creating clear winners and laggards. Accommodation, sports and recreation, and cafes and restaurants are well above their 2019 employment levels, while travel agencies and casinos remain deeply below them; growth is therefore concentrated in experiences, accommodation and longer-stay demand.

Accommodation has 55% more jobs than in 2019, but fewer businesses

More jobs do not automatically mean more businesses. Accommodation has expanded employment while its business count has slightly fallen, suggesting larger or more productive operators; travel agencies show the opposite strain, retaining businesses while losing jobs. Cafes, restaurants, sports and cultural services are the strongest broad-based growers.

Part-time work has grown fastest, especially for men

Tourism employment has grown for both men and women, but the strongest expansion is in part-time work. Men's part-time jobs rose 39%, lifting their share of male tourism employment, while women's part-time share stayed around two-thirds; the industry's recovery is therefore adding flexibility more than conventional full-time capacity.

Business numbers grew in every state, but Sydney and Melbourne hold almost half of them

Tourism businesses are growing across Australia, but the gains are strongest in smaller jurisdictions and remain concentrated in the major cities. The ACT leads state growth at 15%, while Sydney and Melbourne together contain 45% of regional businesses; this points to a recovery that is geographically widespread but economically concentrated.

Tourism has fewer very small businesses and more large ones

The business recovery is favouring scale. Very small tourism businesses fell 15%, while businesses earning $2 million or more grew by roughly a third; the sector is becoming less dominated by microbusinesses and more reliant on larger operators, even though the middle turnover group remains the largest.

Conclusion

Australia's tourism recovery is real, but it is not a return to the old pattern. Employment and peak-season demand have recovered ahead of total international arrivals, while North-East Asia remains below its former strength. The visitors who have returned are more likely to work or stay longer, and the businesses benefiting most are concentrated in accommodation, experiences and larger operators. Tourism is therefore growing again, but its markets, workforce and business structure are being reshaped at the same time.
