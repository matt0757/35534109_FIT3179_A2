Tourism is back, but unevenly

Australian tourism has recovered on paper. Underneath, the picture is more uneven.

Since the borders reopened, is Australian tourism back? This story follows the recovery through visitor numbers, travel patterns, jobs and businesses, and asks three questions along the way.

Have visitors actually returned? Who is coming now, and who is not? And who is benefiting from the recovery, in jobs and in businesses?

1. Have visitors actually returned?

1.1 Jobs are well above 2019, but visitors are not

* Hover over the chart to compare any quarter.

While international visitor arrivals to Australia remain constrained at 90% of pre-pandemic baseline levels (2019), the domestic tourism sector has demonstrated remarkable employment growth, with tourism main jobs surging to 128% of 2019 levels. This significant divergence indicates that workforce demand in the sector is no longer strictly tied to foreign tourist volumes.

Instead, employment expansion is primarily sustained by robust domestic activity, mirrored by returning residents reaching 112% of 2019 levels, alongside broader national employment growth where whole-economy jobs stand at 116%. Overall, domestic travel demand and localized industry adaptation are effectively driving job creation despite the incomplete recovery of international arrival numbers.

1.2 The December peak survived the shutdown, but the quiet months are still quieter

Visitors per month (thousands), with colour showing each month as a percentage of the same month in 2019.
* Hover over a square for details.

The gap is not even across the year either. December 2025 reached 96% of its 2019 level and February 2026 slightly passed its old peak, but June is still at 86%. Peak-season demand is close to normal while the quieter months lag, so the recovery is arriving in bursts rather than evenly.

1.3 The jobs came back long before the visitors did

Overseas visitors (12-month total) and tourism jobs (12-month average), connected in time order.
* Hover over a dot for details.

Tourism jobs were near or above their old level while international arrivals were still recovering, and they kept climbing as visitors slowly returned. Employment, it seems, is not simply following the number of international arrivals, so the next question is who is arriving now.

2. Who is coming now, and who is not?

2.1 New Zealand now leads China in visitors

Top visitor markets ranked by number of visitors, 2019 compared with the year to June 2026.
* Hover over a line or dot for details.

The first answer is in the ranking. New Zealand (1.42 million visitors) has overtaken China (1.13 million), which was the largest source market in 2019, a lead of about 300,000 visitors. China is 22% below 2019 while South Korea is 45% above it, so this is not a general slowdown: some markets have shrunk and others have grown.

2.2 Australia’s visitor map has been redrawn

Overseas arrivals to Australia based on country of residence.
* Hover over a line or dot for details.
* Drag the slider to change the year.

Every market collapsed in 2020–21, but the return since 2023 has not been even. China is still well below 2019, while South Korea and India are already above it. The result is a new map of strong and weak source markets rather than a return to the old order.

2.3 North-East Asia explains most of the visitors still missing

Overseas visitors to Australia by region of residence, 12-month totals (millions).
* Hover over the chart for details.

Following the reopening of borders in February 2022, visitor numbers have steadily rebounded. However, the recovery is asymmetrical: regions like South & Central Asia have grown past previous levels (+0.61M with +0.08 growth), while others remain slightly below pre-pandemic baselines.

As highlighted by the title, North-East Asia accounted for the largest share of visitors prior to 2020. Post-reopening, it remains the largest component of visitors still "missing" compared to pre-pandemic peaks, showing a larger deficit (-0.28) than any other region.

2.4 Fewer holidaymakers and short stays, more workers and long stays

Change in visitors since 2019 by purpose of visit (top) and length of stay (bottom).
* Hover over a bar for details.

So who is filling the gap? The mix of visitors has changed. Holiday and business travel have fallen, while employment-related visits are up 81% and stays of six to twelve months are up 18%. Visitors are coming for work more than for holidays and staying longer, which may be how fewer arrivals can still support so much activity.

3. Who is benefiting from the recovery?

3.1 Seven of twelve tourism industries now employ more people than in 2019

Tourism jobs in each industry compared with its 2019 average.
* Hover over the chart to compare any quarter.
* Use the Industry menu to highlight one industry.

That change in who visits shows up in who is hired. Accommodation, sports and recreation, and cafes and restaurants are well above their 2019 employment, while travel agencies and casinos are still deeply below. The winners are mostly industries that serve people who stay and do things, which fits with the longer-stay pattern above.

3.2 Accommodation has 89% more jobs than in 2020, but fewer businesses

Change in tourism jobs against change in businesses for each industry.
* Hover over a bubble for details.

More jobs do not automatically mean more businesses. Accommodation has expanded employment while its business count has slightly fallen, suggesting larger or more productive operators. Travel agencies show the opposite strain, keeping their businesses while losing jobs.

Cafes, restaurants, sports and cultural services are the strongest broad-based growers. Both measures compare 2025 with 2020, the first year of business counts. Because 2020 was the pandemic low point, job growth looks larger than it would against 2019, and industry matching between the two measures is approximate.

3.3 Part-time work has grown fastest, especially for men

Tourism jobs by sex and full-time or part-time work, with the part-time share, 2019 to the year to June 2026.
* Hover over a bar for details.

Looking inside the jobs themselves, employment has grown for both men and women, but mostly in part-time work. Men’s part-time jobs rose 39%, lifting their share of male tourism employment, while women’s part-time share stayed around two-thirds. The recovery is adding flexible work more than conventional full-time positions.

3.4 Business numbers grew in every state, led by the smaller jurisdictions

Change in tourism businesses by state, 2020 to 2025.
* Hover over a state for details.

Business numbers grew in every state, and the gains are strongest in the smaller jurisdictions, with the ACT leading at 15%. The map starts in 2020, so it does not show the change from before COVID.

3.5 Sydney and Melbourne hold almost half of the businesses

Number of businesses by tourism region in 2025, coloured by change since 2021.
* Hover over a circle for details.
* Choose a zoom centre and drag the zoom slider.

The same unevenness appears in where businesses are. Sydney and Melbourne together hold 45% of regional businesses.

The recovery is geographically widespread but economically concentrated in the big cities. The region map starts in 2021, so it does not show the change from before COVID, and circles sit at approximate central points rather than exact boundaries.

3.6 Tourism has fewer very small businesses and more large ones

Change in the number of tourism businesses by annual turnover band, 2020 to 2025. Rectangle size shows the number of businesses.
* Hover over a rectangle for details.

The landscape of tourism-related businesses has shifted toward larger operators, though mid-sized firms generating $200k–$2m remain the dominant group. Within core tourism industries, microbusinesses earning under $50k experienced a sharp decline of 15%, while larger firms earning $2m or more expanded by over 33%.

Conversely, connected industries saw growth across all turnover brackets, including a 9% increase in microbusinesses. Note that these nominal turnover bands are not adjusted for inflation, meaning some upward mobility across tiers reflects general price increases rather than pure expansion in real terms.

So, is tourism back?

Back to the three questions. Tourism’s recovery is real, but it is not a return to the old pattern.

  • Have visitors returned? Not fully. Jobs and Australians’ overseas travel are above 2019, but international arrivals are still below it, and peak months are close to normal while the quiet months lag.
  • Who is coming now? New Zealand has overtaken China, and South Korea and India are above 2019, but North-East Asia accounts for most of the shortfall. The visitors who have come back are more likely to work or stay longer.
  • Who is benefiting? Accommodation, restaurants and experiences, part-time workers, and larger businesses, with business numbers concentrated in Sydney and Melbourne.
